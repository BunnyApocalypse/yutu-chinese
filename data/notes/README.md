# Learning notes

A short note and an example sentence for each word of a level of a ready-made word list, shown on the word's page in the app: what the word is made of, something worth knowing about using it, and a sentence it occurs in.

One file per level, named like the level's group file (`hsk-3.0-1.tsv` is HSK 3.0 level 1). One word per line, in the order the level is taught, with tabs between the columns:

1. the word in simplified characters
2. its pinyin as the app stores it (tone numbers)
3. the note, in English, with Chinese in simplified characters
4. the example sentence in simplified characters
5. the same sentence in traditional characters (Taiwan forms)
6. the sentence's pinyin with tone marks, a space between words (the app tells the words of the sentence apart by these spaces)
7. the sentence in English
8. how the note was checked, if at all, as words with a space between them: `native` when a native speaker of Chinese has read the note and its sentence (see below). `sources` when every fact the note gives about its characters (meanings of the word and of words it names, readings, parts and where they sit, origin, simplified and traditional forms) was found word for word in Wiktionary's glyph origins, Make Me a Hanzi or CC-CEDICT, by the check described below. Remarks on usage and the example sentence are outside that check. `judged` for a note written checked (below): its facts on how characters were built found in the sources, the rest read by a second AI model. Most lines have no eighth column. The word page shows ✓ beside "Learning tips" for a note with `native` or `sources`, and AI for one with neither, `judged` included, since no person has read it (the owner, 2026-10-28); a tap says which (`docs/word-page.md`).

So far: HSK 3.0 levels 1 (509 words, written as a trial) and 2 (755 words), written on 2026-10-13; levels 3 (931 words), 4 (977) and 5 (1,056), written on 2026-10-14 (level 3 leans on usage more than on how characters are built); `tocfl-novice.tsv`, the 74 words of the TOCFL's two beginner levels that those do not cover; `tocfl-1.tsv` to `tocfl-3.tsv`, the 44, 112 and 301 words of the TOCFL's levels 1 to 3 that none of the others cover (2026-10-14); and `tocfl-4.tsv`, the 1,135 words of its level 4 that none of the others cover (2026-10-14, the same evening). A word has one note, whichever list it is met in. On 2026-10-27, a first 25 notes written a new way (below) for words of the owner's own study that no file covered: `hsk-3.0-6.tsv` (2 words so far), `hsk-3.0-7-9.tsv` (8), `hsk-2.0-6.tsv` (1), `tocfl-5.tsv` (1), and `extra.tsv` (13 words in no ready-made list, in the order of their pinyin; its Chinese in traditional characters). The notes for the HSK levels have the Chinese inside the note in simplified characters; those written for the TOCFL have it in traditional characters and describe Taiwan's usage first.

`radicals.tsv` is of another kind: notes about parts of characters. The first 60 lines are the radicals that occur most often in the lower levels' words (2026-10-13); the other 96 (2026-10-14) complete the deck of radicals (`data/wordlists/radicals/`), so that every radical there that is not a word has a note. Those 96 were written by a Sonnet model from the same sources plus the characters of the lists each radical is in; their examples were then checked by a script against Make Me a Hanzi, keeping only forms that really have the radical (the model had written pairs like 殺/杀 where only one of the two has it). It has the radical in the first column, nothing in the second, and the note in the third, with no example sentence. The app shows such a note on the page of a part of a character, whatever reading the part has. (One was rewritten by hand: 王, which the model, kept to its sources, had described as having no theme; as a radical it is jade, 玉.) These notes were written from what Make Me a Hanzi says of the characters each radical occurs in, and from Wiktionary for the radical's own origin.

## How they were made, and how far to trust them

They were written in October 2026 by an AI model (Anthropic's Claude Sonnet) working to a brief, for this project. For what it says about how characters are built, the model was given two sources and told to use nothing else: the character dictionary of Make Me a Hanzi (`data/makemeahanzi/`) and the glyph-origin sections of the English Wiktionary's entries for the characters. Usage remarks and example sentences are the model's own.

A script checked the form (every word once, lengths, that each sentence contains its word); samples were read by another model and by the project's owner. **No fluent speaker has checked them line by line.** Native speakers have checked only the notes whose writers were unsure, or that make claims about region, register or politeness: a Taiwanese reader on 2026-10-14, and a mainland reader from 2026-10-15. A note may be wrong, and an example sentence may be stiff. The notes they read, with their sentences, are marked `native` in the eighth column: all 80 on the Taiwanese reader's page and the first 80 of the 131 on the mainland reader's page, 160 notes in all (2026-10-24). A reader's correction was worked into the note afterwards in condensed form, so the wording is not always the reader's own. They are meant to be corrected by hand: edit the file.

**The source check** (`sources`, from 2026-10-25; tools and each pass's instructions in the git-ignored `local/source-check/`): a model splits the note into its claims and quotes, for each, a passage from the entries of the word, its characters or the Chinese the note names; a script confirms each quote appears word for word in that entry; a second model, seeing only claim and passage, judges whether the passage says it; a third checks that no fact of the note was left out of the claims; a fourth sorts the claims into facts about the characters and usage. The tag goes on when every fact about the characters passed both the script and the judge, and only after the owner has looked at a sample of the chunk. A scholar's guess passes only if the note calls it uncertain; "often" and the like need support. A claim that failed gets a second search for a quote; that a word is made of the characters it is written with needs no source; and the owner may pass a single claim by hand (three so far: 口 "an opening", the jiāo of 教 on its own, and the apostrophe of nǚ'ér). Checked in chunks of HSK 3.0 level 1, spread over the level: on 2026-10-25 the first 50 (25 passed), then 25 more, the seven corrected after the first among them (all 25 passed, 14 after rewording).

**Written checked, from 2026-10-27** (the files and lines named above as written that day; tools and briefs in the git-ignored `local/notes-writing/`). The writer was given each word's CC-CEDICT and Wiktionary entries and told what the word page already shows (the meaning, the parts and their roles), so that a note never repeats them, and is mostly about use: what the word goes with, register, a near-synonym, and a region only where the word really differs (the owner, 2026-10-27). It wrote each note as labeled sentences: a fact about how a character was built or where it came from carries a quote from those entries, confirmed word for word by a script and judged by a second model (Claude Opus); everything else, the meanings of related words included, was read by that model as a careful native-level reader, who also judged the example sentence. Sentences it questioned went back to the writer, to reword or drop. These notes are tagged `judged`, not `sources`, since that tag's check also covers the meanings of words a note names; they show AI, and a tap says how they were checked (`page.noteJudged`).

A note does not name its sources; this file does.
